\section{Measurement}
The subject of this experiment is the so-called \quotedef{vibrating reed,} pictured in \autoref{fig:setup}.
It consists of a metal plate, clamped down on one end, which is compelled to vibrate.
In the first part of the experiment, we want to measure its first three resonance frequencies \( ν_0 \), \( ν_1 \), and \( ν_2 \).
In the second part, we investigate the dependence of \( ν_0 \) on the temperature of the reed.

\subsection{Experimental Setup and Hardware Architecture}
A brass reed with approximate dimensions of length \( l \approx \qty{20}{\milli\meter} \), width \( b \approx \qty{5}{\milli\meter} \), and thickness \( d \approx \qty{200}{\micro\meter} \) is rigidly clamped at its lower end between two copper blocks.
The free upper end is positioned between two electrostatic circular electrodes of diameter \( D = \qty{3}{\milli\meter} \) positioned at a distance of \( g \approx \qty{100}{\micro\meter} \) on opposite sides of the sample.

The excitation electrode is driven by a synthesized function generator (Stanford Research Systems DS345) supplying an AC voltage \( U(t) = U_0 \cos(ω t) \) with an amplitude of \( U_0 = \qty{10.0}{\volt} \).
Because the electrostatic force exerted on the reed scales quadratically with the applied voltage (\( F \propto U^2 \)), driving the electrode at a frequency \( ν / 2 \) induces mechanical oscillations in the reed at the harmonic frequency \( ν \).

The resulting deflection is detected electrostatically using the opposite electrode, which is biased with a static DC voltage \( U_B = \qty{108}{\volt} \) supplied by a low-noise battery power supply.
Motion of the reed alters the capacitance between the reed and the detection electrode, inducing an AC current that is converted into a voltage signal.
To extract the weak signal from background noise, the signal is routed to a dual-phase Lock-In amplifier (Stanford Research Systems SR530) operating in \( 2f \) harmonic mode.
Operating in \( 2f \) mode ensures that crosstalk from the excitation signal at frequency \( ν/2 \) is effectively filtered out.

The Lock-In amplifier yields two output components:
\begin{equation}
    X = A \cos φ, \qquad
    Y = A \sin φ, \label{eq:lockin}
\end{equation}
from which the signal amplitude \( A \) and relative phase shift \( φ \) are calculated via:
\begin{equation}
    A = \sqrt{X^2 + Y^2},  \qquad
    φ = \arctan(Y / X).
    \label{eq:calc_amp_and_phase}
\end{equation}

Temperature control and readout are integrated directly into the sample holder.
A Peltier element mounted on the copper clamping base allows controlled heating of the sample, driven by an analog control voltage supplied via the auxiliary output (\texttt{X6}/\texttt{out}) of the Lock-In amplifier.
Sample temperature is monitored using a Pt1000 resistance sensor connected to the auxiliary analog input (\texttt{X2}/\texttt{in}) of the Lock-In amplifier.
The measured sensor voltage \( V_T \) is converted to temperature \( T \) in Celsius using the linear calibration curve:
\begin{equation}
    T = (V_T - 1.000325) \times 259.2684
    \label{eq:temp_conversion}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Software Architecture and Device Automation}
The data acquisition and instrument control pipeline in this experiment was implemented in Python using the \texttt{PyVISA} library operating over a Prologix GPIB-USB interface.
This modular architecture provides full automation, structured data logging, and robust error handling.

The software architecture is structured into four primary functional layers.
At the hardware interface level, \texttt{device\_managers.py} defines a \texttt{GPIB} base class for string escaping and address routing, while derived classes (\texttt{SynFunGen} and \texttt{LockInAmp}) encapsulate device-specific SCPI commands.
These are wrapped by a \texttt{Devices} facade class that provides unified access methods to abstract low-level communication from the experiment logic.
Execution parameters such as sample counts, frequency resolution, voltage ranges, and thermalization delays are encapsulated within configuration classes in \texttt{experiment\_configs.py}.
During execution, the high-level measurement loops for frequency sweeps and temperature stabilization are handled by dedicated managers (\texttt{experimentA\_manager.py} and \texttt{experimentB\_manager.py}).
Finally, data persistence is managed by a \texttt{DataWriter} class, which records measured values along with comprehensive execution metadata directly into CSV formats.

\subsection{Measurement Protocols}

\subsubsection{Experiment A: Resonance Spectrum Characterization}
The aim of experiment A is to map the resonance spectra \( A(ν) \) and \( φ(ν) \) across the first three theoretical resonance frequencies (\( ν_0 \approx \qty{125}{\Hz} \), \( ν_1 \approx \qty{825}{\Hz} \), and \( ν_2 \approx \qty{2406.2}{\Hz} \)) at room temperature.

For each frequency, a spectrum consisting of 200 logarithmically spaced points is swept.
At each frequency step, the function generator is updated, followed by a settling delay of \( \Delta t = \qty{0.9}{\second} \) to accommodate the Lock-In integration time constant.
Subsequently, \( N = 20 \) repeated measurements of the quadrature components \( X \) and \( Y \) are sampled.
Individual amplitude and phase values are computed via \autoref{eq:calc_amp_and_phase}.

The sample mean \( \bar{x} \) and the standard error of the mean \( s_{\bar{x}} \) are calculated according to the central limit theorem:
\begin{equation}
    \bar{x} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i, \quad s_{\bar{x}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{N}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N(N-1)} \sum_{i=1}^{N} (x_i - \bar{x})^2}.
    \label{eq:sem}
\end{equation}
The resulting mean values (\( A \), \( φ \)) and their statistical uncertainties (\( A_{\text{std}} \), \( φ_{\text{std}} \)) are stored alongside the frequency value.

\subsubsection{Experiment B: Temperature-Dependent Resonance Shift}

Experiment B investigates the variation of the fundamental resonance frequency \( ν_0 \) as a function of temperature \( T \).
Heating is induced by stepping the Peltier drive voltage across 10 levels from \qty{0.0}{\volt} to \qty{1.0}{\volt}.

Because thermal gradient drifts induce significant frequency shifts, an automated thermalization sequence is executed prior to each frequency sweep.
The protocol begins by setting the target Peltier control voltage on the auxiliary channel \texttt{out}.
To allow the system to respond, an initial thermal wait time of \( t_{\text{wait}} = \qty{300}{\second} \) is enforced.
The manager then performs \( M = 100 \) sequential temperature readings at \qty{1.0}{\second} intervals to assess stabilization.
Thermal equilibrium is confirmed only if both the standard uncertainty \( s_{\bar{T}} = \sigma_T/\sqrt{M} \) and the temperature drift gradient \( \Delta T = |T_{\text{first}} - T_{\text{last}}| \) fall below a \qty{0.5}{\degreeCelsius} threshold.
Should either criterion be violated, the system waits an additional \qty{300}{\second} before re-evaluating.
Once stability is achieved, a frequency sweep of 220 logarithmically spaced points is executed around the fundamental resonance mode and logged.
