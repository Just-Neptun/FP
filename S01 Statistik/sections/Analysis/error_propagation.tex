\subsection{Implementing Symbolic Error Propagation in Python}
\label{sec:analysis:error_propagation}
In this exercise, we make use of the Python library \verb|SymPy| for symbolic mathematics.
\verb|SymPy| lets the user define mathematical symbols, which can be combined into more complex expressions.
We will use \verb|SymPy| to propagate uncertainties of various expressions.

We start with some input variables, \( \vec x = (x_1, \dots, x_n) \), and their corresponding \( n \times n \) covariance matrix,
\begin{equation}
    \symbfup{C}_{ij} = \cov(x_i, x_j) = ρ_{x_i x_j} σ_{x_i} σ_{x_j}.
\end{equation}
Now, we have some functions of these variables, \( \vec f(\vec x) = (f_1(\vec x), \dots, f_m(\vec x)) \), and their corresponding \( m \times m \) covariance matrix,
\begin{equation}
    \symbfup{E}_{ij} = \cov(f_i, f_j) = ρ_{f_i f_j} σ_{f_i} σ_{f_j}.
\end{equation}
Using the (Jacobian) transition matrix,
\begin{equation}
    \symbfup{G}_{ij} = \frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_j},
\end{equation}
we can calculate \( \symbfup{E} \) from \( \symbfup{C} \),
\begin{equation}
    \symbfup{E} = \symbfup{G} \cdot \symbfup{C} \cdot \symbfup{G}^\mathsf T.
\end{equation}

We now implement two functions, \verb|calc_E| and \verb|calc_unc|.
The function \verb|calc_E| uses the above equations to calculate \( \symbfup{E} \) given \( \vec f \), \( \vec x \), and \( \symbfup{C} \).
The function \verb|calc_unc| uses \verb|calc_E| to calculate the covariance matrix of a scalar function \( f \).
It returns the propagated uncertainty, which is just the square root of the matrix's single element.

\subsubsection{Applying Error Propagation to Examples}
\label{sec:analysis:error_propagation:examples}
Starting with the real numbers \( x \) and \( y \) as well as their uncertainties and correlation coefficient \( σ_x \), \( σ_y \), and \( ρ \), their covariance matrix, as defined above, becomes
\begin{equation}
    \symbfup{C} = \begin{pmatrix}
        σ_x^2 & ρ σ_x σ_y \\
        ρ σ_x σ_y & σ_y^2
    \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
First, we derive the absolute uncertainty of their sum and difference, respectively
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
    z &= x + y &&σ_z = \sqrt{2 ρ σ_x σ_y + σ_x^2 + σ_y^2}, \\
    z &= x - y &&σ_z = \sqrt{- 2 ρ σ_x σ_y + σ_x^2 + σ_y^2}.
\end{align}
\end{subequations}

\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
    z &= x y &&\frac{σ_z}{z} = \sqrt{\frac{2 ρ σ_x σ_y}{x y} + \frac{σ_x^2}{x^2} + \frac{σ_y^2}{y^2}}, \\
    z &= \frac{x}{y} &&\frac{σ_z}{z} = \sqrt{- \frac{2 ρ σ_x σ_y}{x y} + \frac{σ_x^2}{x^2} + \frac{σ_y^2}{y^2}}, \\
    z &= x^n y^n &&\frac{σ_z}{z} = \sqrt{\frac{2 ρ σ_x σ_y n^2}{x y} + \frac{σ_x^2 n^2}{x^2} + \frac{σ_y^2 n^2}{y^2}}.
\end{align}
\end{subequations}

Next, we calculate the uncertainty of the acceleration of gravity.
Measuring the length \( L \) and the period \( T \) of a simple pendulum with the corresponding errors \( σ_L \) and \( σ_T \) gives
\begin{equation}
    g = 4  π^2 \frac{L}{T^2}.
\end{equation}
We find the uncertainty to be
\begin{equation}
    σ_g = \sqrt{\frac{64 π^4 L^2 σ_T^2}{T^6} + \frac{16 π^4 σ_L^2}{T^4}} = g \sqrt{\frac{4 σ_T^2}{T^2} + \frac{σ_L^2}{L^2}},
\end{equation}
where we assume uncorrelated variables for simplicity.

Finally, we calculate the energy of a rotating object.
Measuring the angular velocity \( ω \) and the moment of inertia \( I \) with the corresponding errors \( σ_ω \) and \( σ_I \) gives
\begin{equation}
    E = \frac{1}{2} I ω^2.
\end{equation}
We find the uncertainty to be
\begin{equation}
    σ_E = \sqrt{I^2 ω^2 σ_ω^2 + \frac{ω^4 σ_I^2}{4}} = E \sqrt{\frac{4 σ_ω^2}{ω^2} + \frac{σ_I^2}{I^2}},
\end{equation}
where we again assume uncorrelated variables for simplicity.

\subsubsection{Determining the Volume of a Cylinder}
\label{sec:analysis:error_propagation:cylinder}
We will now us \verb|SymPy| for numerical purposes.
The \verb|.evalf()| method substitutes values for any or all variables in a given expression and rounds the result to a specified number of decimal places.
With this we will determine the volume of a cylinder and its associated uncertainty assuming (i) that both measurements are uncorrelated and (ii) that both measurements are fully correlated.

The radius \( r \) and the height \( h \) of the cylinder have been measured to \( r = \qty{5}{\centi\meter} \) and \( h = \qty{2}{\centi\meter} \). 
The uncertainty for both measurements is \( σ = \qty{0.1}{\centi\meter} \). 
We know the formula to be
\begin{equation}
    V = \pi r^2 h.
\end{equation}
If \( r \) and \( h \) are fully correlated, we know that \( ρ = \pm 1 \).
Under assumption (i), we find
\begin{equation}
    V = \qty{157 \pm 10}{\centi\meter\cubed}.
\end{equation}
Under assumption (ii), we find
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
    V(ρ = +1) &= \qty{157 \pm 14}{\centi\meter\cubed}, \\
    V(ρ = -1) &= \qty{157.1 \pm 1.6}{\centi\meter\cubed}.
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
