\section{Critical Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Symbolic Error Propagation}
We made use of the Python library \verb|SymPy| by implementing symbolic error propagation.
First, we created the function \verb|calc_E|, which calculates the covariance matrix \( \mat{E} \) of a function \( \vec{f} \) of a random vector \( \vec{x} \) with covariance matrix \( \mat{C} \).
If \( \vec{x} \) represents the result of a measurement, this gives the associated uncertainties.
This approach is quite general and can handle any (finite) dimension of \( \vec{x} \) and \( \vec{f} \).

One specific case of interest is \( \vec{f} \) being a scalar function \( f \).
We handled this using the function \verb|calc_unc|.
It returns the uncertainty of the value \( f(\vec{x}) \) inherited from \( \vec{x} \).

Using these two functions let us propagate the uncertainties of several specific examples.
While the expressions \verb|SymPy| produced were not always immediately visually appealing, making use of the built-in methods gave rise to a satisfactory result.
Symbolic error propagation using \verb|SymPy| lends itself well to performing tedious calculations, which one would otherwise need to do manually.
Furthermore, the built-in method for variable substitution leverages the numeric capabilities of the Python programming language.
This lets one combine the symbolic and numeric parts of a calculation in one interface, thereby reducing the opportunity for human error.

\subsection{Least Squares Method} 

We used a numerical approach to fit a polynomial to some data. \autoref{fig:least_squares:3rd_polynomial_analytic_and_numeric} shows the difference between analytical and numerical regression approaches. We can see that they generally fit together quite well.

To test how well the fit function models the data, we used a \( \chi^2 \) goodness of fit test. The distribution and test statistic is shown in \autoref{fig:least_squares:chi_square_distribution_1}. The \( p \)-value of the fit was \( \boxed{p \approx \num{0.310}} \).

This value gives the probability of obtaining a $\chi^2$-value equal to or more extreme (larger) than this one, under the assumption that the determined parameters are the true values. Generally hypothesis testing is done with significance levels of \( p > 0.05 \). Because our \( p \)-value is much larger than any reasonable significance level, we \emph{fail} to reject the null hypothesis that the parameters are correct. This goodness of fit test therefore leaves us with fairly high confidence that our fit parameters are quite close to the true values.

We also fitted a linear model. The resulting function and its goodness of fit can be seen in \autoref{fig:least_squares:linear_fit}. As we can also see on the $\chi^2$-distribution graph in \autoref{fig:least_squares:chi_square_distribution_linear_1}, the obtained $\chi^2_{\mathrm{obs}}$ statistic is extremely large compared to the bulk of the distribution. The resulting $p$-value of \( p = \num{3.88e-8} \) is most definitely under the significance level of $0.05$, leading to us \emph{rejecting} the null hypothesis. Therefore, this is \emph{not} an acceptable model of the data. The analyzed data most likely comes from a different (nonlinear) distribution, for example the third degree polynomial which we examined earlier.

However, if the errors in the data are simply made three times larger (see \autoref{fig:least_squares:linear_fit_double_errors}), the \( \chi^2 \) statistic suddenly looks much better. Because of the reduction in \( \chi^2_\mathrm{obs} \), the \( p \)-value is increased significantly (\( p = 0.224 \)).

Using a naive analysis, the high \( p \)-value indicates that the probability of the data varying at least this much from the model due to random error is \num{0.224}. Because this is quite likely, we would gain a lot of confidence in the validity of the model.

This calculation shows how estimating errors is a very important part of data collection. It is clear that a third degree polynomial is a much better model of the data, but by simply increasing the errors other models seem to fit as well. Overestimating errors is therefore also harmful.

To mitigate this problem, real data analysis can also include looking at the residuals (difference between measurement and model prediction) for each data point. In this case, with a linear fit, it may be visible that the residuals are consistently more positive for low values of \( x \), while high values of \( x \) cause low and even negative residuals. This corresponds to what is seen visually on \autoref{fig:least_squares:linear_fit_double_errors}: The model is consistently too high on the left while too low on the right.

Finding a pattern in the residuals like this means the measurements are not randomly distributed around the model prediction, and that there is some kind of systematic problem, indicating that the model is not correct for the data set. It does not capture the shape of the data properly.


\subsection{Maximum Likelihood Estimator Method}

We also used the MLE method to estimate parameters of model distributions. The resulting graphs are given in \autoref{fig:mle:lllh_30_data_points}.

We see that as number of samples $n$ increases, the log-likelihood (as well as its approximation) gets much more strongly peaked (note the changing $y$-axis scales). The maximum likelihood is correctly predicted by both calculations, because the approximation is a Taylor expansion around the MLE. Increasing the sample size means the approximation becomes better near the maximum. However, the log-likelihood function clearly does not globally approach a quadratic, as we can see that the two sides remain very asymmetric, even at large sample sizes.

Therefore, the parabolic approximation is valid within a region around the maximum likelihood, even though the function is more complicated than this when viewed globally. This was expected due to the parabola being calculated from the Taylor expansion of $\ln L(\tau)$ around the maximum.

We ran an experiment to calculate how successful the log-likelihood method is for estimating the true value of \( \tau \). The success rate was \qty{67}{\percent}. This success rate is our estimate/expectation for the probability of a successful prediction of \( \tau \) when using the MLE method.

Analyzing specific numbers of samples, clearly very small samples have very large errors and have no power to accurately determine a parameter $a$. The errors are not just large, but also unbalanced: The difference between $\sigma^+$ and $\sigma^-$ is greater than $5 \%$ for small samples.

The $\hat{a}$ estimate appears to converge relatively quickly. However, we see that even reducing the sample number by only $10$, to $n = 990$, the difference between $\sigma^+$ and $\sigma^-$ already grows to over $5 \%$. This value seems to converge much more slowly.

We can also observe that (at least with our dataset) the difference in upper and lower uncertainties does not monotonically reduce with sample size. For example, from $200$ to $750$ samples the difference increases by about $4$ percentage points. From $n = 995$ to $n = 996$ the difference also jumps up, although after this point it reduces sharply. At the maximum number of samples, we include the entire dataset and $n = 1000$. At this point the difference in errors is only $1.4 \%$.

Interestingly, the \texttt{minos()} method finds very different values of $\sigma^+$ and $\sigma^-$, leading to a much smaller difference in their values overall. Their values have a difference of below $5 \%$ for all sample sizes above about $n = 50$.


\subsection{Applying Bayes' Theorem}

We applied Bayes' theorem to estimate the chance of a particle decaying to a specific channel. It allows us to update our beliefs when given more and more data. The probability distribution of the parameter $f_\alpha$ is given for 1, 5 and 100 decays in \autoref{fig:bayes_theorem:posteriors}.

As was expected and is logical, getting more samples increases our confidence in predicting the true value of a parameter, so the distribution gets sharper, and the peak converges to a certain position.

We successfully applied Bayes' theorem using a robust method with hyperparameters to efficiently calculate and predict \( f_\alpha \).
