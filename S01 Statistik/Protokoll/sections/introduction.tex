\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
Before we can focus on error propagation and parameter estimation, we need to introduce the basics of probability theory.

\subsection{Basic Probability Theory}
We formally model a random process using a \define{probability space}.
This is a triple consisting of a \define{sample space} \( Ω \), an \define{event space} \( F \), and a \define{probability function} \( P\colon F \to [0, 1] \).
An \define{outcome} \( ω \in Ω \) is a possible result, an \define{event} \( E \in F \) is a set of outcomes, and \( P(E) \) is the \define{probability} of an event occurring.

An event \( E \) occurred if the result \( ω \) of the process is in \( E \), \( ω \in E \).
Since \( ω \in Ω \), we know \( P(Ω) = 1 \).
If two events \( E_1, E_2 \) are \define{mutually exclusive}, \( E_1 \cap E_2 = \emptyset \), then the probability of either one happening is the sum of their individual probabilities, \( P(E_1 \cup E_2) = P(E_1) + P(E_2) \).
The \define{conditional probability} of event \( A \) occurring, given the information that event \( B \) with \( P(B) \neq 0 \) has occurred, is 
\begin{equation}
    P(A \given B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}.
\end{equation}

A \define{random variable} \( X \) is a function from the sample space to the real numbers, \( X\colon Ω \to \reals \), and its values \( x = X(ω) \in \reals \) are called its \define{states}.
We naturally define the \define{image} \( X(E) = \set{X(ω) \in \reals \mid ω \in E} \) of an event \( E \) and the \define{pre-image} \( X^{-1}(A) = \set{ω \in Ω \mid X(ω) \in A} \) of a set of states \( A \).
The probability that \( X \) takes on a state in \( A \subseteq \reals \) is given by its pre-image,
\begin{equation}
    P(X \in A) \coloneq P(\set{ω \in Ω \mid X(ω) \in A}) = P(X^{-1}(A)).
\end{equation}

\subsection{Probability Mass and Density Functions}
A random variable may be either \define{discrete} (if \( X(Ω) \) is at most countably infinite) or \define{continuous} (if \( X(Ω) \) is uncountably infinite).
A discrete random variable admits a \define{probability mass function} (PMF) \( p_X\colon \reals \to [0, 1] \), which gives the probability that \( X \) is in state \( x \in \reals\),
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:introduction:definiton_pmf}
    p_X(x) \coloneq P(X = x) = P(X^{-1}(\set{x})).
\end{equation}
A continuous random variable admits a \define{probability density function} (PDF) \( f_X\colon \reals \to \reals \), which gives the probability \emph{per unit length} of finding \( X \) in state \( x \in \reals \),
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:introduction:definition_pdf}
    f_X(x) \coloneq \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{P(X \in [x, x + h])}{h} = \eval{\diff{F_X}{x}}{x}.
\end{equation}
Here we implicitly defined the \define{cumulative distribution function} (CDF) \( F_X\colon \reals \to [0, 1] \), as 
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:introduction:definition_cdf}
    F_X(x) \coloneq P(X \leq x) = P(X^{-1}((-\infty, x])).\footnotemark
\end{equation}
\footnotetext{
    This definition satisfies \autoref{eq:introduction:definition_pdf} since \( F_X(x + h) - F_X(x) = P(X \leq x + h) - P(X \leq x) = P(x \leq X \leq x + h). \)
}%
It follows that integrating the PDF over an interval gives the probability of finding the random variable in that interval,
\begin{equation}
    P(a \leq X \leq b) = \int_a^b f_X(x) \difl{x}.
\end{equation}
Furthermore, \( f_X \) is non-negative and normalized on \( \reals \).

\subsection{Multiple Random Variables}
A \define{random vector} \( \vec{X} \) is a generalization of a random variable to \( N \) dimensions, \( \vec{X}\colon Ω \to \reals^N \).
It can be understood as a vector of random variables, \( \vec{X} = (X_1, \dots, X_N)\transpose \).
Its states are vectors in \( \reals^N \), and it can be discrete or continuous.\footnote{
    We disregard the possibility of there being cross-terms, for example \( X_1 \) being discrete and \( X_2 \) being continuous.
}
We can similarly define PMFs, CDFs, and PDFs:
\begin{gather} 
    p_{\vec{X}}(\vec{x}) \coloneq P(\vec{X} = \vec{x}) = P(\vec{X}^{-1}(\set{\vec{x}})), \\
    F_{\vec{X}}(\vec{x}) \coloneq P(\vec{X} \leq \vec{x}) = P(\vec{X}^{-1}((-\infty, \vec{x}])), \\
    f_{\vec{X}}(\vec{x}) \coloneq \lim_{\vec{h} \to \vec{0}} \frac{P(\vec{X} \in [\vec{x}, \vec{x} + \vec{h}])}{\prod_{i} h_i} = \eval{\diffp<n>{F_{\vec{X}}}{x_1,x_2,...,x_n}}{\vec{x}}.
\end{gather}
The notation is to be understood component-wise, i.e. \( \vec{X} \leq \vec{x} \iff \forall i \in \set{1, \dots, N}\colon X_i \leq x_i \).

Again, \( P(\vec{X} \in M \subseteq \reals^N) = \int_M f_{\vec{X}}(\vec{x}) \difl{\vec{x}} \), with the PDF being non-negative and normalized on \( \reals^N \).
A \define{marginal distribution} is achieved by integrating (or summing, in case of a PMF) over all but one dimensions,
\begin{equation}
    f_{X_1}(x_1) = \int_{\reals^{N-1}} f_{\vec{X}}(\vec{x}) \difl{x_2} \cdots \difl{x_N}.
\end{equation}
We call two random variables \define{independent} if their \define{joint distribution} factorizes,
\begin{equation}
    f_{X,Y}(x,y) = f_X(x) \cdot f_Y(y).
\end{equation}

\subsection{Mean, Variance, and Covariance}
The \define{expectation value} of a function \( g\colon \reals^N \to \reals \) of a random vector is
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:introduction:definition_expectation}
\begin{alignat}{2}
    \ev{g(X)} &\coloneq \sum_{x \in X(Ω)} g(x) p_X(x) &&\qquad\text{if \( X \) is discrete} \\
    \ev{g(X)} &\coloneq \int_{\reals} g(x) f_X(x) \difl{x} &&\qquad\text{if \( X \) is continuous}
\end{alignat}
\end{subequations}
From this, we define the \define{mean} and \define{variance} of a random variable as
\begin{subequations}
\begin{gather}
    \label{eq:introduction:definition_mean}
    \mean(X) = \mu_X \coloneq \ev{X}, \\
    \label{eq:introduction:definition_variance}
    \var(X) \coloneq σ_X^2 \coloneq \ev{(X - μ_X)^2} = \ev{X^2} - μ_X^2.
\end{gather}
\end{subequations}
The last equality follows from the fact that \autoref{eq:introduction:definition_expectation} implies the linearity of the expectation value, \( \ev{aX + b} = a \ev{X} + b \).
If there are multiple random variables, we use the marginal distributions to determine the mean and variance of each variable.
The square-root of the variance is called the \define{standard deviation}.

The \define{covariance} of two random variables is defined as 
\begin{equation}
    \cov(X, Y) \coloneq \ev{(X - μ_X)(Y - μ_Y)} = \ev{XY} - μ_X μ_Y.
\end{equation}
If the covariance is not zero, this means that the two variables depend on each other. 
We normalize it to receive the \define{correlation}
\begin{equation}
    ρ = \frac{\cov(X, Y)}{σ_X σ_Y}.
\end{equation}
If \( ρ \) is close to \( 1 \) or \( -1 \), it reflects that \( X \) taking on large values will also tend to correspond with \( Y \) taking on large values. 
A small \( ρ \) means large \( X \) corresponds to small \( Y \).
A positive value for \( ρ \) means that \( X \) and \( Y \) tend to be on the same side of their respective mean, while negative \( ρ \) means the opposite.

\subsection{The Gaussian Distribution}

To complete the introduction, we will discuss some relevant probability distribution functions. These will obviously be needed in the Analysis section later. Some simpler distributions will also be introduced later, but these are the most important and interesting ones to discuss in more detail here.

The Gaussian distribution is one of the \quoteapprox{standard} distributions in statistics. It is defined as follows.

\begin{gather}
    P(x; \mu, \sigma) = \frac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2 \pi}} e^{\frac{(x - μ)^2}{2 \sigma^2}} \\
    \text{Mean: } \ev{x} = \mu \\
    \text{Variance: } \sigma_{x}^2 = \sigma^2
\end{gather}

This distribution is often assumed as a kind of \quoteapprox{default} for many randomly distributed variables in nature. This is due to the \define{central limit theorem}. It states that for the average value \( X = 
\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n x_i \), when \( n \rightarrow \infty \), the distribution of \( X \) approaches a Gaussian distribution.

Because measurements are influenced by the addition of many different statistical errors (which behave as randomly distributed variables), it is usually assumed that a measurement comes from a Gaussian distribution centered around the true value (mean \( \mu \) of the distribution) with an error \( \sigma \) (the square root of the variance \( \sigma_x^2 \) of the distribution).

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The \( \chi^2 \)-Distribution}{The Chi-Squared-Distribution}}

If \( n \) random variables \( x_1, \dotsc, x_n \) all follow standard Gaussian distributions with \( \mu = 0 \) and \( \sigma = 1 \), the sum of their squares follows the \( \chi^2 \)-distribution for \( n \) so-called \quotedef{degrees of freedom}.
\begin{gather}
    z = \chi^2 = x_1^2 + \dotsb + x_n^2 \\
    f(z; \, n) = \frac{z^{\frac{n}{2} - 1} \cdot e^{-\frac{z}{2}}}{2^{\frac{n}{2}} \cdot \Gamma{\big( \frac{n}{2} \big)}} \quad (z ≥ 0)
    \label{eq:introduction:chi_square_distribution}
\end{gather}

This is relevant for analyzing the quality of a model relative to measured data, as the differences between prediction and measurement should be distributed around \( \mu = 0 \). If it is assumed that the measurement errors cause a Gaussian distribution of the measurement values, then the width of the distributions can also be normalized to \( \sigma = 1 \), producing \( n \) Gaussian distributions.

When analyzing a model, the number of fit parameters for the model must be subtracted off from the total number of measurement points to obtain the degrees of freedom. Intuitively, this is because a model with \( n_F \) parameters (such as a polynomial) could perfectly capture the that many data points, causing a deviation of \( 0 \) for all of those measurements. Deviation from the model due to statistical errors can only occur for the \quoteapprox{leftover} data points when the model has fewer parameters than data points, meaning degrees of freedom \( f = n - n_F \).

The differences between prediction and measurement are sometimes called residuals, leading to the name for this test statistic \( \chi^2_{obs} \): \quotedef{the (normalized) sum of the square residuals}.
